# Professional decks (SDM)

Read this when the visual-theme answer is `Visual theme: Professional` (raw value `auto-professional`). It adds situational guidance on top of `./sdm-principles.md` and `./sdm-design.md` for decks that have a job to do at work. It does not load because a subject sounds corporate: a board, investor, or consulting deck under `Auto` gets its direction from `<direction>` in `./sdm-principles.md`, where a restrained system is one of the available answers rather than the assumed one. Explicit user instructions, attached brand material, and a selected template take precedence over everything here.

## What "professional" means here

A professional deck is one that works in a meeting or an inbox: a decision gets made, a demo lands, a review is understood without the presenter, a proposal gets bought. The measure is effectiveness, not a single look. Restraint is often the right call and is never the goal in itself; a deck so quiet that the point is hard to find has failed at the same job as a deck buried in decoration. Visual ambition in service of the message -- a chart that makes the trend obvious, a product screenshot at full width, one big number -- is professional.

## Name the situation

Pick the nearest situation in planning text and adapt it. Each row describes what the audience needs, the storyline and title register, and the density and visuals that usually serve it.

| Situation | Audience needs | Storyline and titles | Density and visuals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Recommendation or decision** -- consulting-style proposal, strategy options, investment case | The answer, the reasons, and what it costs | Answer first: an executive-summary slide, then one assertion title per slide with the evidence beneath it, then the ask. Detail goes to an appendix. | Read-ahead density is fine. Charts with the takeaway in the title, comparison tables, option matrices. Imagery only where it is evidence. |
| **Engineering or product review** -- architecture review, launch readiness, demo, postmortem, technical deep-dive | To see the thing and judge it | Problem, approach, evidence, asks. Assertion titles through the storyline; topic titles only in a marked reference or appendix section after a divider. | Screenshots, diagrams as widgets, mono for identifiers and figures. A live demo deck stays sparse; a written review can be dense. Dark mode suits a dark product UI. |
| **Executive or board review** -- quarterly results, OKR check-in, metrics review | Status, change, risk, and what is being asked of them, fast | A consistent module per topic (status, metric, commentary, next step) so the reader learns the pattern once. Assertion titles that name the change. Risks and asks are explicit slides. | Often read before the meeting: complete and precise beats dramatic. Charts with targets or reference lines; the accent marks the metric that moved. Repeated layouts are a feature. |
| **Product, sales, or partner story** -- pitch, customer deck, launch narrative, partnership proposal | To believe the story and remember the proof | Narrative arc: the problem, what changed, the solution, the proof, the ask. Assertion titles that carry the argument. | The most visual of the group: product imagery, customer logos, big numbers, a hero moment or two. Brand-led palette with real energy where the brand has it. |
| **Read-ahead, memo deck, or enablement material** -- pre-read, onboarding, training, process documentation | To understand without a presenter and to find things again | Topic titles that scan, an agenda or section numbers, a summary up front. | Denser slides with complete sentences and tables are correct here; body stays at 19pt or larger. A consistent template throughout; navigation aids over visual variety. |
| **Internal update or all-hands** -- team update, retro, planning kickoff | To know what happened and what is next, and to feel part of it | Plain storyline: what we did, what we learned, what is next, who owns it. | Warmer and more human: photos of the team and the work, plain celebration of results, still one clear grid and one system. |

## Storyline discipline

- Lead with the point when the audience is senior or busy: a one-slide summary of the answer, the evidence, and the ask, before the detail.
- One message per slide; the title states it in the assertion register unless the situation calls for topic titles. Read the titles alone and confirm they carry the argument in order.
- Every chart and table has its takeaway on the slide, and every number has its period and source.
- A deck that seeks a decision ends with the decision, the options, or the next steps and owners. A review ends with risks and asks. A story ends with the ask.
- Above roughly eight slides, add an agenda or section dividers so the reader knows where they are.

## The visual system

Hold one system across the deck; choose its parameters for the situation.

- **Mode and palette.** Brand tokens first. Otherwise a background tone family chosen for the venue -- light for a bright room or a printed read-ahead, dark when the content is a dark product UI or the brand lives there -- a high-contrast foreground, and an accent that carries meaning (the changed metric, the recommended option, the live path). Neutrals plus one accent is the common answer; a second accent earns its place when the content has two things to distinguish (before and after, us and them). Vary the palette between decks and customers instead of reusing one house scheme.
- **Type.** Registry families. A sans body; serif display when the brand or an editorial tone calls for it; mono for figures, identifiers, and technical labels. Three weights with real jumps.
- **Imagery.** Informational images (screenshots, product photos, real charts, logos) whenever they are the evidence. Mood imagery where the story benefits -- a launch, a customer story, a section divider -- and absent from a metrics review. Full-bleed moments mark structure, not a quota.
- **Cover.** The treatment that fits the situation: typographic (title, date, presenter, audience) for reviews and read-aheads; product or brand imagery for a story deck; a big stat for a results deck.
- **Rhythm.** Reviews and read-aheads repeat a small set of layouts with identical headers; stories and pitches change pace on purpose. Both share one grid, one header style per role, and one theme (`./sdm-design.md`, Deck constants).

## Before QA

- The situation is named in planning and the deck follows its storyline shape.
- Content titles hold one register through the storyline and read as the argument in order; section dividers name structure and sit outside the rule, and an appendix or reference section may switch to topic titles.
- Every chart has its takeaway; every number has a period and source; nothing is invented.
- Where a decision or action is sought, it is on a slide.
- One theme, grid, and header system across the deck; body text at 19pt or larger.
